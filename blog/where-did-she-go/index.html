From the Front Seat — No. 2

Where Did She Go?

A caregiver who loves her work told me this week scared her: she came home, looked in the mirror, and couldn't quite find herself in her own life.

By Greg Woolf · October 13, 2026

Tuesday morning, a little after seven, a woman got into my car who spends her days taking care of an autistic boy.

She loves the work. She said it more than once, and I believed her every time. "I love to impact people," she said. "I love to help people, you know?"

But loving it wasn't the whole story. "I didn't realize until this week," she told me. "It kinda scared me. Like yesterday."

The Mirror

I've been trying to get into the habit of asking, "So, what are you looking forward to today?" So after this question, my rider paused quite thoughtfully.

She told me about coming home the day before. The boy she looks after likes to mess her hair up — all the time, she said — so by the end of the day her hair was pretty much however he'd left it. She walked into her house, caught sight of herself in the mirror, and just stopped.

"I'm coming back home and I look in the mirror," she said, "and I'm just like — what is happening?"

"He's autistic, you know," she goes, "so it's a lot of layers."

The clothes were part of it too. Every day now she dresses for comfort, for the job — whatever survives the day. It didn't used to be that way. "I like fashion," she said, a little like she was confessing to something. She used to get dressed up. Somewhere along the way, getting ready for her own life got quietly replaced by getting ready for somebody else's.

Her one day off is Sunday, and Sundays she goes to church — so the week is spoken for before it ever starts. She'd just been on the phone with a friend, actually reaching out, trying to put a lunch together. "I haven't been reaching out to my friends," she said.

She wasn't complaining. That's what struck me. She was taking inventory. "I love it," she goes, "but I'm like — okay. Where am I in this? You know?"

A Servant, Not a Serving

I told her what I tell anybody who'll listen in this car: there's no shame in having a limit. The shame is in never finding out where it is. And sometimes the limit isn't a stop sign — sometimes you find the challenge in it, and you let the challenge drive you to the answer.

I told her about a friend of mine who was in deep at her church — every activity, every job they had to hand out, she had one; sometimes up to three. Rewarding work, and she meant that. But she told me one day that the work will devour you if you let it, and you reach a point where you have to sit down and decide: is this helping me, or is it only helping everybody else? Isn't "losing yourself in the work" designed to help you find yourself, your bliss?

Because there's a difference, and I said it to her right there in the car, because it might be the truest thing I know about helping people: it's one thing to be a servant. It's another thing entirely to be served up. One of those is a calling. The other is just disappearing in slow motion.

Even the commandment says it plain, if you read the whole sentence. Love your neighbor as yourself. The "as yourself" is in there for a reason. You have to appreciate yourself before you can love anybody else well — treat yourself badly long enough and the serving runs dry, resentment builds; no matter how much you love the people you're serving. She hadn't stopped loving the work. She'd just noticed she'd stopped appearing in her own life, and she was asking the right question about it. There's nothing wrong with asking the right questions. Nearly every answer I've ever found started out as one, but more importantly, the right one.

The Same Sentence, Twice in One Morning

Here's the part I keep turning over.

That same morning — 3:49 a.m., hours before she got in — I'd had nearly this exact conversation in the dark with another rider, somebody sharing their testimony with me (the common formula: Jesus Christ = Lord & Savior). And I'd quoted the same sentence: "Love thy neighbor as thyself. So you have to love yourself." I told that rider what I believe: folks who don't have a real connection with somebody tend not to treat themselves very well, and once they connect, they start wanting to be the best they can be. I think God (The Father) wants us to understand this about our human nature. It's a trifecta of sorts, love yourself, love others, love God. One without the others can cause a multitude of "connection" problems. Disconnected things that don't have power, tend to lose in various areas, namely: energy, strength, fuel, purpose.

Then the sun came up, and a caregiver got into my car and asked me where she had gone in her own life.

Two riders. One morning. The same sentence, walking in from both directions.

She got out of my car still loving her job — she wanted to be clear about that. She wasn't quitting anything. She was refusing to disappear from the life she spends on everybody else. And I don't think that's selfish. I think that's the commandment, read all the way to the end of the sentence.

Love your neighbor as yourself was never a command to vanish. "As yourself" is half the commandment — and you can't love anybody for long out of a life you never show up in yourself.
