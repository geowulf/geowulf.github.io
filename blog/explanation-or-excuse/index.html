From the Front Seat — No. 7

Is That an Explanation or an Excuse?

My wife asks one question better than anybody — is that an explanation, or is that an excuse? A long ride to Terminal 4, and everything it opened up.

By Greg Woolf · November 10, 2026

A long ride to Terminal 4 started with the tablet on the back of my seat — and ended with the question my wife asks better than anybody.

My mornings start around 3 a.m. — I've been driving about eight years. This Wednesday I picked up a couple headed to Terminal 4, a long ride, and lucky: they were talkers. The kind of ride where you forget to check the clock.

They started with the tablet on the back of the seat — second version they've sent out, a Samsung with rideshare software on it, twenty bucks a month for keeping it charged. One of them goes, "I wouldn't walk past twenty bucks if it were laying on the road." Fair enough.

But the tablet got us talking about something better. I told them about a line my wife uses — on everybody, including me. Somebody was explaining why they hadn't done a thing they said they'd do, and my wife goes, "Is that an explanation or is that an excuse?"

Because they are different things.

The excuse machine

Here's what I said in the car, and I believe it: technology was invented to give us all excuses.

"My computer's down." "I lost my signal." "No Wi-Fi." That's the answer to everything now. The tools are real. The problem is what we do with them.

I told them about a young woman I know. Got out of the Army, needed a job, didn't have a phone. So she sat in the parking lot and waited for them to come get her when they needed her. No app, no signal, no excuse. You do what you have to do if you really want something bad enough.

That's the difference between back then and now. When we were young adults, it wasn't okay to go back and live with your parents. You had to make it on your own. Now it's like you're forty before you move out.

My daughter called from the Midwest — she was going to move out here, go to school. Her mom called me too: "I'm so glad she's going out there, because then you're gonna take care of her." And I said, "Well, if we raised her correctly, she can handle it all herself." The youngest came out from Michigan, and we thought she'd live with us forever. She was out in a month, working on her own. Some of this generation still has it in them. It's just not very common.

Are you your job?

Somewhere around the freeway we got onto AI, because of course we did. And I said the thing I actually believe: I don't think AI is going to do anything to us as much as we're going to forfeit a lot of things to it. We've already started — our attention to social media, the effort to be curious and actually examine something. There's no research anymore. It's too easy to just ask a tablet a question.

We talked about Black Mirror — the episode where everybody rates everybody, and the one where a chip in a soldier's head makes the enemy look like monsters until it glitches. Even though we can, should we?

We touched on worth and value, too — a thing I'd been chewing on all morning. Worth is given. Value is assigned. Worth answers "Do I matter?" Value answers "How much does this matter — and to whom, right now?"

And then one of them asked the question that stuck with me the rest of the ride: with everybody scared AI is going to take their job — are you your job? Because if you take away my job, and I don't know any other way to say who I am... what am I?

I said it out loud: that probably would have been me. If I'd been terminated before my career was over, I probably would have lost my mind. But I think work is an eternal principle — it's always going to be there, in one form or another.

They owned it

I used to work for a wilderness treatment program — we'd take kids into the wild, teach them primitive skills, keep them off drugs, help them rebuild their sense of worth. Most of the time, the real problem was the parent didn't want to deal with the kid, so they just handed over money. And the kid wanted attention.

But the kids who got it — really got it — grasped this idea of radical honesty with themselves. They understood that every consequence they absorbed was their own. So they owned it.

That's a gift to give anybody. And the flip side is just as true: if you choose the wrong path, you know exactly why you did.

The kicker

By then we were close to the terminal, and I told them about the guy I picked up from a party once. He fell asleep in the back, and by the time I dropped him off it had gotten light out. He wakes up: "Where's my girlfriend?" I said, "Dude, you're the only person who got in the car." He looks beside himself: "No — she was sitting right up there in the front with you." And then: "Wait — where's my best friend?"

They laughed, and one of them goes, "It's gonna give you a lot of really good stories, having a job like that. Has a driver who's been driving as long as you have ever written a book of stories?"

And I go, "I have the stories. I just haven't compiled them yet. I gotta get on the ball with that one."

An explanation tells you what happened. An excuse tells you why it wasn't your fault. The difference is whether you still own it — and nobody can forfeit that for you. Not a computer, not a phone, not a machine. Only you.

So here I am. Compiling.
