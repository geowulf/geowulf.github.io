From the Front Seat — No. 3

Starting Over at Sixty

She changed careers at sixty, lost her husband, and got robbed by somebody she was trying to help. What she does for a living now is the part I can't stop thinking about.

By Greg Woolf · October 20, 2026

Wednesday morning, a little after seven-thirty, a woman got into my car and I asked her my new question for riders: what fun things are you looking forward to today?

She goes, "You never know in my job."

You Never Know in My Job

She works for Circle the City — healthcare for the homeless. I asked if it was mostly emergency care, or the other kind — the kind that comes from things being let go too long.

She said it's both, and then some: illness, mental illness, neglect. Wounds that never healed right. And medications — people's medications get stolen all the time out there, like it's just a fact of the weather.

I asked if she'd done it her whole career.

She goes, "I switched careers at sixty years old."

Before that: sales and marketing, and real estate, in Chicago. That's not a career change. That's a whole different life, traded in at an age when most people are counting down to retirement.

What Brought Her to Arizona

What brought her to Arizona was her husband's health. I said that probably led straight into the work she does now — and that's where she stopped me.

"I don't really even want to talk about it," she said.

So we didn't. A rider doesn't owe you the hard parts. She said, "No, you're okay" — and then she gave me the shape of it anyway, in her own order.

It's coming up on six years since she got to Arizona. "October twentieth I got off the airplane, and it was a shit show for four years."

Her husband died. And in the middle of all that, she helped a girl out — and the girl got into her account and stole twelve thousand dollars, her rent money. She had six dollars left. Six. And she couldn't get it back, because technically she had given the girl the number herself.

One thing after another, for four years. I told her life can't really be summed up in a nutshell — but if you only have a brief amount of time, that was about the size of it.

A Story for a Story

When somebody hands you the hardest four years of their life in a nineteen-minute ride, you don't top it. You trade. So I told her one of mine.

Five in the morning, airport runs all morning, and I got a pickup at a hotel. One man on a bench, hood up, half asleep — he gets in the back. Just as I'm pulling out, another guy runs out, jumps in the front seat, and goes, "Hey man, give me a ride up the road."

"I don't know who you are. My rider's this guy here."

He turns to the back seat: "Hey, can you tell your driver to give me a ride?" The man in the back wakes up and goes, "Yeah — help my buddy out."

"Up the road," he kept saying. "Just up the road." So I drove. Is it here? No, keep going. Is it here? Keep going. Finally he hands me his phone — and the phone says the W Hotel. Which is where we had just come from. He goes, "I parked right behind it."

Behind it. We'd driven away from the very place he was parked.

Then he can't find his keys. I find his car — a white Chevy Malibu — and I said, "I know where your keys are." He goes, "Where?" I said, "In that white Chevy Malibu with the lights on. The car's running."

And the whole ride he kept asking where his phone was — the phone in his hand. I told him, "It's in your hand," and he didn't believe me.

At his car, he pulls out a roll of twenties and goes, "How much do I owe you?" I said about a hundred bucks would do it. He goes, "That's kind of steep." I said, "Look at it this way — when I find your phone, I'll give it to you." He goes, "Oh — you'll do that for me?" And he happily handed over the hundred dollars. For a phone he was holding.

She laughed. I dropped her off, she told me to be safe, and she went in to work.

What She Kept

Here's the part I keep turning over, days later.

She got robbed by somebody she was trying to help — twelve thousand dollars, down to six, rent money gone. And what does she do with what's left of her life? She goes to work taking care of people whose medications get stolen all the time. She didn't come out of those four years harder. She came out of them employed — at sixty-something — by the exact kind of trouble that was done to her.

Most of us learn the lesson the other way: help somebody, get burned, swear off helping. Her way runs the opposite direction. She got off an airplane into the worst four years of her life, lost her husband, lost her money to a person she was kind to — and decided the answer was to help people for a living anyway.

You never know in her job. She told me that in the first thirty seconds, and by the end of the ride I understood: that's not a complaint. That's the whole job description — and apparently, the whole life.
