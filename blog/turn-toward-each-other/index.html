From the Front Seat — No. 8

Turn Toward Each Other

A wedding trip during a nationwide grounding, a neighbor who found out exactly who he was talking to, and the one question that decides whether a marriage lasts.

By Greg Woolf · November 17, 2026

Saturday morning, the whole country couldn't get where it was going. Flights grounded nationwide — one rider told me it was a radio-frequency problem messing with aircraft control, grounded for safety, no terrorism link anybody knew about. The hotels were sold out, the few rooms left going for around a thousand a night, and all morning I kept getting ride requests from people who wanted to go way up north or way down south, anywhere the planes weren't.

My airport run that morning was a woman headed to the American Airlines terminal, in town for her niece's wedding. Her niece did a stretch in the Air Force and now works at Boeing — her husband, too. She'd spent the day before out with her cousins and counted four or five bachelorette parties wandering around town.

We stopped at a 24-hour spot on the way so she could grab something to eat. She ordered part of it in Spanish, and I almost talked myself into a waffle.

I didn't mean you

I told her a story I tell in this car more than any other — about a neighbor of mine, a man from Mexico City, came from a pretty prominent town down there, thick accent and all. He married a woman who looks about as Irish as they come — freckled face, red hair — and she speaks Spanish, too.

One day he's out mowing his lawn, his wife on the porch, real pregnant, just watching him work, and a new neighbor comes by, starts chatting with her while the mower's running, and then he gets going about Mexicans infiltrating the neighborhood.

She's just nodding, not saying a whole lot.

Her husband sees what's happening, turns off the mower, walks over, takes his hat off, sets his bandana down, and reaches out his hand. "Hey, neighbor."

And the guy just turns white. Whiter than he already was. "Oh — no, I'm sorry. I didn't mean you."

And she just grins.

He didn't argue with the man. He didn't lecture him. He just walked over and let the man see who he'd been talking to.

Twice the work

Somewhere on the freeway we got onto technology. I told her what I keep hearing from riders: AI is a great tool, except now they do twice as much work, because they have to check everything it spits out. One made-up line in the wrong document and you're done.

I saw a video of a senator in a hearing who started reading the chatbot's answer back to him, right there on camera, before he caught himself and set it down.

And it's not just AI. A retired cop told me once that when he started on the force, if you wanted to make a call you pulled over and found a pay phone. By the end they handed him three phones, one for each job, and right before he retired they took all three away and gave him one phone that did everything. He goes, "Why didn't we just do that in the first place?"

The computer's down. The internet's down. No Wi-Fi. That's the answer to everything now, and everybody accepts it. I've sat in video meetings where the person I'm talking to is sitting right next to me. People walk past my front porch staring at their phones, not looking up, not talking to anybody.

Technology was supposed to bring us closer. Mostly it's given us a really good excuse not to look at the person right next to us.

Turn toward

When I was younger I worked at a wilderness treatment program. We'd take troubled kids into the woods, teach them survival skills — and then we'd work with the parents, too, and that was the part that mattered. Because most of those kids weren't bad kids. They were kids trying to get their parents' attention — and the best way to get attention is negative attention.

The program I worked for was a good one. But for every good program, there's about fifteen just trying to make money off somebody else's kid, and after a while the good people throw their hands up and walk away.

A man said something to me once that I've never forgotten. He goes, "If you're married to somebody and your whole goal in an argument is to be right — okay, you win. One person's a winner and one person's a loser. Do you want to be married to a loser?"

There's a man named John Gottman — him and his wife have studied relationships for forty years, they call it the Love Lab — and what they keep coming back to is this: when there's a problem, it's not your problem or her problem. It's everybody's problem. You turn toward each other and solve it together. Too many people turn away.

And no — the answer isn't just always saying she's right, either. That's not turning toward somebody. That's giving up.

The whole job

I gave her directions to the American Airlines counter, and she went off to catch her flight home from a wedding, in a week when nobody could get anywhere.

Here's what I know. The neighbor on the porch didn't change anybody's mind with an argument. The kids in the woods didn't get better because somebody won a fight with them. And no marriage ever got stronger because one of them scored a point.

You turn toward the person next to you. That's the whole job. The rest is just the ride.
