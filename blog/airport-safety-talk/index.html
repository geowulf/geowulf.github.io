From the Front Seat — No. 5

The Airport Safety Talk

A rainy airport ride turned into a safety discussion — and safety, it turns out, is a question, not a lecture.

By Greg Woolf · November 3, 2026

It was raining on Halloween, the kind of Arizona rain that makes everybody drive like they're guessing, and I had a long airport ride. Somewhere in that ride the conversation turned to safety.

Not safety as a lecture. Safety as a question. That's usually how it goes in my car — somebody says something about how they do things, and I hear the teaching underneath it. Somebody taught them that way. The question is whether what they were taught actually keeps them safe.

The pickup dance

If you've never watched an airport pickup from the driver's seat, here's the dance. The app tells you where to go. The rider is somewhere else. The curb is full, the rain is coming down, and everybody is waving at a car that isn't theirs.

Search first. That's the first beat of SEE, and it's the whole job at an airport. Where's my rider? Where's a place I can actually stop without blocking traffic or getting run over? Where are the people walking between cars with suitcases, looking at their phones instead of the road?

I spend half my airport time not driving. Just looking. Because the pickup is the most dangerous part of the ride, and nobody treats it that way. Everybody treats the highway as the dangerous part. The highway is predictable. The curb is not.

Is it safe? Is it effective? Is it efficient?

Those are the three questions I run everything through, on the bike and in the car. At the airport they sort themselves out fast.

Is it safe? Can I stop here without somebody walking into my door or somebody behind me having to swerve? Is it effective? Will stopping here actually get my rider in the car, or will they be standing on the wrong island watching me? Is it efficient? Because a safe, effective stop that takes ten minutes and three loops around the terminal isn't efficient — and an efficient stop that puts somebody in traffic isn't safe.

Most people only ask one of the three. The hurrier asks "is it fast?" The rule-follower asks "is it allowed?" The worrier asks "is it safe?" and then freezes. The trick is asking all three at once, in about five seconds, in the rain, with a line of cars behind you.

That's not a talent. That's training. I teach it on the range every month, and I practice it at the airport every week.

What people were taught

Here's the part that sticks with me. Everybody in that airport was taught safety by somebody, and you can see the teaching in how they move.

Some people were taught that safety means hurry — get in the car fast, get out of the way, don't be the person holding everybody up. So they run across three lanes of traffic with a suitcase, because being quick feels safer than being careful.

Some people were taught that safety means trust the system — stand exactly where the app says, even if that spot is a puddle in a bus lane, because the system knows. They won't move ten feet to a safer curb because the dot on the screen says here.

Some people were taught that safety means look out for yourself and nobody else — and they're the ones who open a door into traffic without looking, because in their teaching, the world is supposed to look out for them.

None of them are bad people. They just got different lessons. And the car is where you see it, because the airport strips away everything polite and shows you what somebody actually does when they're tired, wet, and trying to get home.

The conversation

So when the conversation on that long ride turned to safety, I didn't have a speech. I had a question, the same one I always have: what were you taught?

Because that's the Wayteller question, really. Not "are you safe?" but "who taught you what safe means?" A rider coach, a parent, a job, a scare you had once — somebody handed you a set of moves, and now you run them without thinking. The front seat is where I get to hear which moves somebody picked up, and whether those moves still serve them.

I think about that every time I circle a terminal now. Search, evaluate, execute. Is it safe? Is it effective? Is it efficient? Ask all three, and you don't just get a better pickup. You get a look at how somebody was taught to move through the world.

And if what they were taught isn't keeping them safe, that's not a lecture. That's a conversation. That's the whole ride.
