From the Front Seat — No. 4

Pie Night

One pie per group. That's the whole rule — and somehow it's enough to make a neighborhood.

By Greg Woolf · October 27, 2026

Every year, the day before Thanksgiving, we throw a party with one rule.

One pie per group.

That's it. That's the whole invitation. The whole neighborhood is invited, and if your group wants to bring something, bring one pie. Not one per person — that is TOO MUCH PIE. One per group, whatever your group is. There's no hard and fast rule for what counts as a group. A household, a couple, a carload of friends — you decide. The point of the rule isn't to police anybody. It's to keep the table from collapsing under forty pies.

One pie per group

I like rules that are simple enough to explain in one sentence. One pie per group is that kind of rule — except it's less a rule and more a guardrail. It keeps the good problem, too much generosity, from becoming an actual problem: too much pie.

And "pie" is whatever you believe a pie should be. Apple, pumpkin, pecan, something chocolate that disappears first every single year — or your favorite dish that never saw a crust in its life. You define it. Nobody's checking your crust credentials at the door.

But here's the bigger idea, and it matters more than the rule: come for the company, the fellowship, and the friendship. The pie is the excuse, not the price. If you happen to want to share your favorite pie or dish, bring that too. If you just want to come and be with your neighbors and friends, come. Somebody will hand you a fork.

The table ends up looking like a map of the neighborhood. People stand around with plates and argue about crust the way other people argue about football. Neighbors and friends who wave at each other all year finally learn each other's names over a second slice.

Weird and dangerous

One neighbor told me straight up that it was weird. Dangerous, even.

Dangerous. I turned that word over for a while. What's dangerous about pie?

Here's what I think he meant, even if he didn't say it: it's dangerous to open your door. It's dangerous to trust that people will show up, that they'll bring something real, that the evening won't be awkward or disappointing. Most of us were taught to keep to ourselves. Don't bother people. Don't let people in. Wave from the driveway and call that community.

Pie Night breaks that teaching on purpose. You walk into somebody's house and sit down with people you barely know, and for one night, the neighborhood stops being a collection of addresses and starts being a group of people who feed each other.

People don't care about their neighbors and friends... We do.

That's the line I keep coming back to. People say nobody cares about their neighbors and friends anymore, like it's weather — like it's something that happens to us. But caring isn't weather. It's a decision, and it's a small one. One pie per group. One night. One table.

Nobody at Pie Night gives a speech about community. Nobody has to. The table does the talking. You can see it in the empty plates: people showed up, people shared, people stayed.

This November

We're doing it again this November. Same deal. One pie per group — or just come for the company.

If you've got a neighborhood, you've got everything this takes. You don't need a committee or a budget or a perfect house. You need a date, a table, and the nerve to invite people in.

Come for the company. If you've got a favorite pie — whatever a pie means to you — bring it. But the pie was never the point. The neighbors and friends were.
