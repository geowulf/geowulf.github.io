From the Front Seat — No. 6

Arizona on the Move

Arizona isn't a place people are from — it's a place people move to. And the front seat is where you hear what that move is really about.

By Greg Woolf · November 9, 2026

Everybody in my car is from somewhere else. Or going somewhere else. Sometimes both, in the same ride.

That's Arizona. It's not a place people are from — it's a place people move to. And the front seat is where you hear what that move is really about, because nobody tells you they're starting over. They tell you where they're headed, and you hear the rest underneath it.

What people carry

Here's what I've noticed: when people move, they don't travel light. They bring the teaching with them.

Somebody taught them what home means. Maybe home was a house you never leave. Maybe home was wherever your people were, and your people scattered. Maybe home was a set of rules — how to talk, how to work, what to want — and they packed those rules in a suitcase along with everything else.

So they get to Arizona and they rebuild the same life in a new zip code. Same job, same arguments, same Sunday. The scenery changed. The teaching didn't. And they wonder why the fresh start feels familiar.

I hear that in the car. Not as a complaint — as a fact. "I moved here for a change," somebody says, and then they describe a life that sounds exactly like the one they left. The move was real. The change is still pending.

What people leave behind

Then there's the other kind. The ones who moved to leave something, not to find something.

They don't always say what it was. A town that got too small. A family story that didn't fit anymore. A version of themselves they outgrew. They drove west until the rearview got quiet, and now they're here, figuring out who they are without the audience they grew up performing for.

Those are my favorite rides, if I'm honest. Not because the story is dramatic — because the person is mid-sentence. They haven't finished becoming whoever they're going to be here. They're still deciding which parts of the old teaching to keep and which parts to set down in the desert.

That's a brave place to be. It's also a lonely one. Starting over means nobody around you knows your history, and that cuts both ways: nobody knows your failures, but nobody knows your wins either. You're free, and you're unknown, in the same breath.

The question underneath

So when I drive around this state, I keep coming back to the same question, the one I always come back to: what were you taught?

Were you taught that home is a place, or that home is people? Were you taught that moving on means running away, or that it means growing up? Were you taught that starting over is a failure, or that it's the most honest thing a person can do?

Because Arizona doesn't just have newcomers. It has people mid-move — mid-thought, mid-life, mid-teaching. And the front seat is where you hear the next line before they've said it anywhere else.

I don't have an answer for them. I have a car, a question, and a state full of people who are still writing their sentence.

And if you're one of them — if you moved here, or you're thinking about it, or you're still carrying a teaching that doesn't fit the life you're living now — well. Tell us your way.
