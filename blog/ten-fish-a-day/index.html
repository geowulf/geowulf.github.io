From the Front Seat — No. 9

Ten Fish a Day

A caregiver on her way home from a shift, a coworker who never stops working, and a fisherman who already had everything the businessman was selling.

By Greg Woolf · November 24, 2026

Sunday morning, I picked up a caregiver on her way home. She does care work — and if you drive people to and from their jobs the way I do, you meet a lot of caregivers. A surprising number of them came here from Africa, from Senegal and places like that, and their whole experience of the United States is caring for everybody else.

She told me about a coworker of hers. This woman works almost every day, picks up every extra shift she can get, and sends the money home to her family. A family she doesn't get to see.

That's a lot of dedication, I told her, for a family you don't see. And she just nodded, like yeah, that's the deal. The hope is one day they'll be together, or she'll go back. But in the meantime, she works.

The fisherman

It reminded me of a story I heard once, and I've told it in this car more times than I can count.

There's this fisherman in a little town on the Gulf of Mexico. Every morning he takes his pole down to the water, catches maybe ten fish — big fish — and that's his day. He sells some at the market, feeds his family with the rest, and sometimes he takes a guy out fishing with him. Then he spends the rest of the day with his family, doing whatever he wants.

Well, one day a businessman goes fishing with him, and he goes, "How many fish did you catch today?"

"About ten, on average."

And the businessman goes, "Well, if you had a boat, you could catch more fish."

And the fisherman goes, "Why would I want a boat? I fish in the morning, I spend the rest of the day with my family. I've got enough."

"Yeah, but if you had a boat, you'd make more money. And then you could get two boats, and hire people, and build a whole business. And then you'd be a manager, and you could travel, and make bigger deals —" and he just keeps going, bigger and bigger and bigger, until one day, he says, when you've made all these millions of dollars, then you can retire. Then you can get a little shack by the water and fish all day and do whatever you want.

And the fisherman just looks at him and goes, "But I already do that."

Sometimes people chase a dream, and it's not even their dream. You shouldn't chase a dream just for the ambition alone.

You can't buy somebody into your dream

I know a dentist who learned that the hard way. He couldn't find staff, so he went to his nieces and nephews, his friends, and he said, "If you go to school, I'll pay for it. All you've got to do is promise you'll come work for me after." And a lot of them said no. No, I don't want to do that. He couldn't find anybody — he couldn't even pay people to go to school to do it.

Ambition doesn't transfer. You can offer somebody the whole ladder, and if the top of it isn't something they actually want, they'll walk right past it.

How do you propose I do that?

There's a book I read about the art of negotiation, written by a guy who was an FBI negotiator. And he talks about how negotiation changed — it used to be all demands and proof of life, and now it's about getting the other side to solve the problem they created. Somebody makes a demand — a million dollars and a car, whatever — and instead of arguing about it, you just go, "How do you propose I do that?"

Now they're thinking about a problem they created. Either they solve it, or they realize it can't be done.

I use that one at home, too. My kids will come to me and go, "I want this, I want that," and I'll go, "Okay. I'm glad you want that — it's a good thing to desire stuff in your life. But how do you propose you're gonna get that?"

And they go, "Oh, well, I thought you'd just give it to me."

Well, why would that happen?

My rider that morning had figured that part out on her own. She told me she'd learned to do things for herself — nobody handed it to her — and now everybody wants her to help them. And I told her, that's good. That's the part to hold on to. Because sometimes if nobody can help you, the only thing you're gonna do is help yourself.

And as for everybody who wants your help now? She goes, "Well, they can figure it out."

I goes, "Exactly. How much help did they give you?"

"Well, that's as much as I'm gonna give you."

The whole job

I dropped her off and she went on with her day. And I kept thinking about the fisherman.

He wasn't lazy. He wasn't unambitious. He just already knew what the businessman was still trying to buy — mornings on the water, and the rest of the day with the people he loves.

Ten fish a day, and a life that's already his. That's not settling. That's knowing what you're fishing for.
